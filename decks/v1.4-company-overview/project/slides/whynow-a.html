What is changing

Conventional real estate investment structures were designed by and for institutional investors, and families who invest through them have found the terms opaque, rigid, and transactional.

More families are forming offices of their own, typically lean ones led by a chief investment officer who covers every asset class and has little room to specialize in any one of them.

Listings and general market data have become a commodity, so the edge has moved to off-market opportunities and ground-level insight that travel through relationships.

Transactions too large for individual investors and too small for institutional funds draw few bidders, and that band has widened as institutional funds have grown.

What it means for families

Families now seek structures built around their tax position, their holding period objectives, and their wish to choose what they own.

Real estate rewards local knowledge, proprietary sourcing, and hands-on management, the specialist capabilities a lean office would need a dedicated team to build.

A family investing on its own sees the opportunities its own relationships bring and compares notes with the peers the family already knows, and a network of like-minded families widens both.

Families who commit equity together can compete in that band and win on certainty of execution.
